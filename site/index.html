The gist

Six dials that sum up the US economy and stock market. Green (OK) means nothing unusual, amber (Watch) means a warning sign is active, red (Alert) means the measure is at a level that has historically meant trouble or high risk. The arrow under each number shows which way it moved since the previous reading. Tap any dial to see what it means, why it matters, the numbers behind it and its history. These describe where things stand today; they are not forecasts or advice.

Markets at a glance

Each row follows a whole market through one large fund (an ETF) that holds it, because fund prices are free and update daily. The price shown is the price of one share of the fund, not the index level: SPY holds the 500 companies of the S&P 500 and trades near one-tenth of the index; QQQ holds the Nasdaq-100; VEA holds large companies in developed countries outside the US (Europe, Japan, Canada, Australia); VWO holds emerging markets such as China, India, Taiwan and Brazil; AGG holds the broad US bond market; TLT holds long-dated US Treasury bonds; GLD holds physical gold, with one share worth a little under a tenth of an ounce. These funds track their markets closely, so the percentage changes are a good guide. Day, 1m and YTD are the change over one day, one month and since the start of the year. Trend is ▲ when the price is above its average of the last 200 trading days.

T-bills and rates

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short loans to the US government, from 4 weeks to 1 year. Their yield is the interest you earn, and it is the closest thing to a risk-free return on cash. Longer loans (2 to 30 years) are called notes and bonds. The yield curve compares short and long yields: normally long ones are higher. When short yields are higher the curve is “inverted”, which has often come before recessions. A basis point (bp) is one-hundredth of a percentage point. The Treasury sells new bills at auctions most weeks.
